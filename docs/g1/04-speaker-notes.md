## Slide 1: Before the Cut

Before the Cut is a game about deciding whether a patient is safe for cybernetic surgery. You play a doctor who installs mechanical body parts. It's inspired by Cyberpunk 2077 and its fictional condition, cyberpsychosis, where people with extensive implants can lose control. The tension comes from examining someone who might attack you.

## Slide 2: How a patient visit works

Your reference sheet shows how a patient should normally look and behave. You can inspect them, ask them to perform an action, or talk to them, even to provoke a reaction. You choose which checks to use. If they won't release a handle, for example, you can investigate further before choosing surgery or calling Trauma Team, an emergency response unit. If a patient attacks, you can close the safety shutter.

## Slide 3: Players, motivations and pillars

I'm aiming at Cyberpunk fans and players who enjoy spotting clues. Fantasy comes from being the doctor; Discovery comes from understanding each patient. Sensation comes from the suspense of a possible attack. My design priorities are readable clues, freedom to choose checks, and patients with different personalities.

## Slide 4: Concepts and references

I first considered an island mystery: you repeated the same day, using earlier discoveries to find your missing sister. But it needed too many locations and story events. A shop about inspecting and pricing objects felt too close to Probably Stolen. The clinic gives me a smaller setting, with visual comparisons inspired by That's not my Neighbor.

## Slide 5: The patient agent's role

The patients are where AI comes in. Each one responds using their personality and conversation history, so I don't have to write every answer. I'll compare this with scripted dialogue to test whether patients feel more believable and whether their reactions affect your decisions.

## Slide 6: How the game and agent work together

The diagram shows that exchange: your action and conversation go to the AI, and its reply comes back to the game. Technical checks handle errors, while the game still controls physical evidence and outcomes.

## Slide 7: Scope and budgets

For this small prototype, I'm targeting one-second replies and a two-cent budget per shift, based on twelve AI requests. If a reply takes longer than two point five seconds, a prepared reply keeps the game moving. I haven't measured this with a real model yet.

## Slide 8: Risks and mitigations

That small scope also addresses my biggest risks: this is my first game, and I'm working alone. I'll cut decorative polish first and bring in playtesters.

## Slide 9: Prototype approval and next steps

I'm asking for approval to build and test this version. I chose the clinic setting and wanted players to decide how to examine each patient. If the clues confuse players, or AI dialogue does not affect their decisions, I'll revise before adding content.
