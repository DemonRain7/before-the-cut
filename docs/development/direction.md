## Look: "Lamplight Noir"

- **Canvas.** 640 x 360 pixel art, shown at whole multiples (2x at 1280 x 720, 3x at 1080p). One screen, no scrolling. [Student: Godot 4, 2D pixel art, clinic counter.]
- **Palette.** About 60 named colours in fixed ramps. Teal-ink shadows against one sodium-amber desk lamp. Cream paper for documents. Phosphor green for the hall camera. Magenta and red are reserved for the street neon and for danger, so a red flash always means "act now".
- **Staging.** [Student, November 16: for immersion keep only the counter on screen, one large view of it with the visitor across it, and turn everything else into things on the desk or the walls that are clicked open.] You sit in a booth behind a counter window that takes most of the screen (440 x 300 of the 640 x 360; the view through it is drawn at twice its earlier size). The patient stands on the far side, lit by your lamp, with the hall door and the rain behind them. Two strips of booth wall and the desk frame the window. On the left wall hang the reference sheet and tonight's contact list on one hook (since November 17), the lender's meter (the till against the line for this hour, and the queue) and a small monitor of the hall camera with the next visitor's silhouette. On the right wall hang the intake card on its clipboard, the counter guide booklet (in the place of the HOUSE RULE sign, removed on November 17 to make room for the list), and a plate with the stamp, the call box, the shutter lever and the key that orders new glass. The desk carries the three instruments, the transcript unit and the three ways of talking. A tool fills the window with a close-up. A steel shutter rolls down in the window frame.
- **Patients.** Large-headed busts with strong silhouettes, built from layers (eyes, lids, brows, mouth, two hands) so they blink, glance, talk, tap, clench and follow a light. Each has one signature idle that is characterful but is not, by itself, the answer.
- **Interface.** Diegetic and paper-first: nothing is on screen that is not a thing in the booth, and nothing is open that the doctor did not take down. The Normal Reference Sheet is a form pinned to the left wall, tonight's contact list hangs under it, and the patient record is an intake card on a clipboard on the right wall; a click (or R, L, N) takes one down in front of the window, and its tab, the same key or a right-click hangs it back. [Student, November 17: pass and fail were unclear, because nothing pointed at the sheet.] Every tool close-up holds the sheet up beside it: a SHEET line says what the sheet calls steady for that tool, the penlight gives each eye a plain readout (TIGHTENS, STAYS WIDE or IMPLANT; FOLLOWS or DOESN'T FOLLOW), and the grip's scale marks the sheet's limit, LIMIT 1s. The readouts say what happened, never steady or drifting. The hall camera's small monitor switches a larger screen on (C). TREAT is a rubber stamp, Trauma Team is a call box, the shutter is a lever, new glass is a key with its price on it. What is said appears on a transcript unit set into the desk, and that is also where you type. The papers step aside for a tool close-up, the card comes down by itself when it is needed (for the stamp, and for the findings after the visit), and nothing hangs in front of the window once a patient has turned. One original bitmap font. No red/green verdict labels anywhere during an exam.
- **Sound.** Synthesized, small and dry: rain and mains hum, a door chime, a different voice-blip pitch per patient, pencil scratch for notes, a stamp thunk, a radio click and a siren for Trauma Team, a two-tone alarm and a heavy shutter slam, a servo and a seal for the arm that fits new glass. The attacks add a saw or a cutting torch, cracking glass and a heartbeat. No listening test of any of it is recorded yet.
- **Violence.** [Student: make the attack scene wilder and bloodier.] A patient who turns hits the glass, cracking it further each time; if the shutter is still up they come through. The damage of the night stays on the glass and the shutter until the next shift, and it counts: the pane takes three blows a night, and each one that lands leaves less time at the next attack, unless the doctor pays to have a layer put back between patients (see Danger). `--less-blood` plays the same scenes with no blood: no splatter or pools, and the attacker, the cut in the shutter and the broken glass are drawn clean. The red flash and the red edge of the screen stay, because they are the danger signal. Captures of the current build, drawn on the evening of November 17 (local evidence, not in the repository): with blood in `validation/2026-11-17/captures-full/`, without in `validation/2026-11-17/captures-attacks-less-blood/`.
- **Assets.** All original, generated by scripts in `tools/art/` into `game/assets/`. No third-party art, fonts or audio.

## Play: compare, choose, decide

**Premise.** Cyberpsychosis starts as *drift*: the implant begins to move before the person does. Before you cut, you screen for it. (Invented rules for this game, inspired by the Cyberpunk setting; not canon, not medicine.)

**The Normal Reference Sheet** [Student: a sheet of normal states to compare against] has five lines. Each says what a normal patient does, and what ordinary causes do *not* count. This is the text in `game/data/sheet.json`.

| Line | Normal | Ordinary, not a sign |
|---|---|---|
| EYES | Pupils tighten in light. Both eyes follow it. | Implants don't tighten. They still follow. |
| HANDS | Lets go on 'release', inside one second. | Shakes, twitches and a weak grip. |
| PORT | Calm skin at the socket. No dark threads. | Burnt pins, scars, cheap cable marks. |
| SPEECH | Says 'I' and 'my'. Remembers what they did. | Nerves, rudeness, a skipping voice box. |
| TEMPER | Pushed, face and voice react together. | Anger, swearing, walking out. |

House rule printed on the sheet: *Two broken lines = drifting: call TRAUMA TEAM. Strange is not sick. A refusal is not a sign.* The number is `threshold` in `sheet.json`; every drifting case breaks at least that many lines, and the data check refuses a case file that does not.

**Methods** [Student: all optional, any order, skip freely, decide at any time]:

- **Look** at eyes, hands or port by clicking the patient. Free.
- **Read** the reference sheet or your own intake card whenever you like: both hang on the booth walls and come down on a click. Free.
- **Tools**: sweep a penlight across the eyes, run a squeeze-and-release on the grip bar, ask to see the port.
- **Talk**: ask one of three questions, reassure (CALM, paid since November 17), or apply verbal pressure. Or say it in your own words: type a sentence, or hold a key and speak it.
- **Codes**: SAY a code from tonight's contact list to the visitor you take for its contact (see The contacts).
- **Decide** whenever you like: stamp TREAT, or call TRAUMA TEAM to take the patient away [Student: Trauma Team replaces the old referral]. Space drops the shutter at any moment.

**Cost.** Everything you ask of a patient costs patience; looking costs nothing. [Student, November 15: a patient whose patience is gone should leave by themselves, with no fee and no fine.] The action that spends the last lamp still happens. Then the patient says they are going, a countdown of eight seconds runs in place of the lamps, and they walk out: the hour is lost, nothing is paid and nothing is billed. A decision can still be made during the countdown. Reassurance (twice a visit) gives one lamp back, turns a refusal around and, during the countdown, holds the patient at the counter. [Student, November 17: no CALM, or CALM costs money.] It is now a calm-patch at 30 credits a use, so it trades credits for a longer exam; the till may go below zero for it. This replaces the old check counter, strain and universal re-check.

**Reading results.** The game shows what happened (an animation in the close-up, a neutral caption, a line on the intake card). It never labels a result normal or abnormal. The player compares it with the sheet. [Student, November 15: keep the automatic notes and let the player tick or cross on top of them.] Findings are still noted on the intake card by themselves, also while it hangs on the wall, where a small number counts the notes not yet looked at; a box beside each row takes the player's own tick or cross (the boxes are older than the note; the guide and a one-time hint now point at them). New on November 16: the card counts the crosses against the house rule (`CROSSED 1/2`), and the strip says so when the count reaches it. The count is advice: nothing scores a mark and nothing stops a decision.

**Truth and the patient** [Student: fixed truth; the patient AI decides its own replies and reactions]. Each patient is fixed as steady or drifting. What an instrument shows, how a patient's face and hands react to pressure, who attacks and how every decision is scored are fixed in the case files and played by the game.

- *With a patient AI service*, every spoken line and visible reaction is written by the model from the character's background, personality, motives and the conversation so far. There is no answer list, no dialogue tree and no replacement of what it says. It may refuse a test. The intake card records what it actually said. Only shape, length and time limits are checked.
- *Offline* (the default, and the fallback when a call fails), the patient follows an authored script. This is the comparison condition. With a service connected, the corner of the window says whether the last line came from the AI, from the mock transport or from the offline fallback.
- The patient can talk, stall and refuse, but cannot change what the instruments show. The reaction to pressure is always played by the game, so every drifting patient has at least one broken line that cannot be talked away.

**Your own words.** The typing line and push-to-talk feed the same conversation. Speaking does not send anything: the words land on the typing line, where you read them, fix them and press ENTER, or take them back. The microphone is open only while the key is held, nothing is recorded to disk, and what you say is treated as the doctor's line in the scene, never as an instruction to the model. Push-to-talk is offered only when a patient AI service is connected; typing and subtitles always work. The patient does not speak aloud.

**Trauma Team.** The call box takes two presses, because the call cannot be taken back. Dispatch answers, a siren arrives, two crew step in behind the patient and walk them out. It is an authored outcome with fixed lines, not another AI agent and not a combat system. A needless call on a steady patient is billed to you.

**Danger.** Some drifting patients attack: one when Trauma Team is called, both when pressed twice. The warning is always visible and audible, and the time to react starts only when it appears. [Student, November 15: make the defence a timing moment, and let the glass wear down.] A red bar over the printer slot then fills from the warning to the moment the patient is through; its notches are the blows on the glass (at 0.54, 0.99 and 1.44 s of a 1.8 s window). SPACE drops the shutter. Before the first notch the stop is clean. Every blow that lands first costs the pane one of its three lamps for the rest of the night, and with fewer lamps there are fewer blows before the break-through: 1.44 s with two left, 0.99 s with one, 0.65 s with none. The plate that takes the bar's place says how it went. [Student, November 16: let the player pay to repair the counter glass that earlier visitors damaged.] Between patients, while the next one waits in the hall, 40 credits put one layer back (the GLASS key on the right wall, or G), up to a whole pane; an empty frame gets glass again. The money comes off the till, the till may go under zero for it, and the lender's meter counts it at every later reading: safety is bought with the same credits the line wants, and a whole pane (120) costs half an hour of the line. Calling Trauma Team does not by itself cause an attack, and not every drifting patient is violent.

**Snaps and flares.** [Student, November 17: during the eye test, the grip or any other action, the patient may suddenly fly into a rage and lunge.] A drifting patient may snap in the middle of any action: nothing for the first three actions of a visit, then 8, 16, 24 and at most 30 per cent an action. An open close-up is knocked out of the doctor's hands, the patient surges at the glass, and the ordinary attack starts 0.35 s later. A long exam of a drifting patient is therefore dangerous, and knowing when to stop and decide is part of the game. Anyone may flare once a visit: a shout and a palm on the glass, no red bar and nothing to stop. A flare is evidence for TEMPER only (a steady patient's face and voice go together, a drifting one's do not), and dropping the shutter on it is a false alarm. Neither happens on the training visit.

**After each patient.** A printed slip shows the outcome, the truth, and all five lines for that patient: what each would have shown and whether you saw it. Then the lender's meter reads the till against the line for that hour, and one line says who is waiting in the hall.

**The lender's line.** [Student, November 15: give the till a meaning; the doctor is squeezed too, and so much an hour keeps them off the cut line.] The doctor's chair, tools and own two hands are on lease from a lender, Harrow Credit (an invented name). One patient is one hour. The meter wants 240 more in the till every hour (120 until November 17), so hour n needs n x 240 and the night needs 1,200. A reading under the line is shown in red and counted, but the shift goes on; only the last reading decides the night. Under the line at closing is the ending called THE CUT, whatever else went right. Five right calls make 1,140, so the night is held only with at least one drop from the contacts (300 each): the list is part of the night's goal, not a side errand.

**The contacts.** [Student, November 17: the doctor, fired by a megacorp and in debt, works for a gang at a street implant clinic; contacts come in to exchange codes and smuggle military or high-end implants; a daily contact list is used to cross-reference the visitors; a wrong code costs patience, and each contact has a different code.] The doctor fitted chrome at Kessler (an invented megacorp) until security walked them out; Kessler sold the debt to Harrow Credit; the clinic belongs to a gang, the Lanterns (invented). Two of each night's five visitors are Lanterns contacts. Tonight's list gives each an alias, two clues and a code line. The clues use only the intake card and a look at the eyes; each clue alone fits at least two visitors and the pair exactly one, which the game checks when it loads its data. SAY speaks a code: the right contact hands a package over (+300, whatever the call), anyone else gives a blank look in their own words and loses 1 patience. A code is not a test of the patient: contacts are steady and drifting alike, and the drop changes neither the truth, nor the call's credits, nor the chance of an attack. Codes are game-owned: the patient AI never hears one.

**The opening, the training and the guide.** [Student, November 15: a short opening that gives the background, and a tutorial or clear feedback. November 17: the grip and the eye test were confusing; the opening should walk the player through everything once.] Once a session, after the title, six cards with small moving pictures tell the story (FIRED, THE DEBT, THE LANTERNS, THE LINE, THE DRIFT, BEFORE THE CUT); any click moves on and ESC skips them. On the first night a small TRAINING box then gives 14 tips, one at a time, each with a pulsing frame round the thing it is about: the list and its clues, the hall camera, the intake card, a code and a drop, the sheet, the three tools, the talk pads, the marks, the decision, and on the first slip what snaps and flares look like. NEXT moves on and K skips them all; nothing blocks and nothing sets an order. The four-page guide, which before November 17 opened by itself at the first patient, opens with H, F1 or the booklet on the right wall. One-time hints appear when they first matter. None of this is a step the player has to take.

**While the patient thinks.** [Student, November 15: show the visitor thinking while the agent works.] While a reply is on its way the patient looks away in their own manner and a small thought bubble sits by the head; while the microphone is open they watch the doctor. Both end when the reply or the clip does.

2. **Rook Dunmore**, dock loader. Hostile, a hand that spasms, a scorched wrist socket. Looks like trouble; it is a burnt cable. Steady.

4. **Juno Reyes**, street singer. Display eyes, chrome jaw, a voice box that skips. The most "cyber" person tonight and perfectly fine. Steady.
5. **Maren Holt**, night-shift welder. Kind, tired, honest about losing time. A natural pupil that never tightens; a hand that holds on 1.7 seconds after "release". Drifting; she goes quietly with Trauma Team, but pressing her twice is a mistake.

Numbers (fees, patience, penalties, the hourly line, the eight seconds, the blow times, the three points of glass, the 40 credits for a layer of it, the threshold of two, and since November 17 the 300 a drop, the 30 credits for CALM and the snap and flare chances) are first-pass values in `game/data/rules.json`, `sheet.json` and the case files; none has been tuned with players.

## Conversation limits: the tradeoff

Raised on November 14 so that a typed or spoken conversation is possible: 16 patient replies per visit (was 4), a 4-second wait before the authored line is used (was 2.5), replies asked at 110 characters and refused only over 220 (was a hard 110), and up to 24 spoken clips of at most 10 seconds per visit.

- More replies means more provider calls: a five-patient shift at the cap is 80 patient calls, and each spoken sentence adds one transcription call.
- The earlier planning target was USD 0.02 per shift, estimated for about ten calls. Eighty calls would be about USD 0.03 by simple proportion, so the new cap is above that target; about ten replies per visit would meet it. That is arithmetic on an old estimate, not a price. Keeping 16 or lowering it is a decision for the student.
- A longer wait means fewer authored fallbacks and a longer worst-case pause at a locked counter.
- The November 15 live run measured 30 patient replies and ten synthetic-speech transcriptions: dialogue median 1.32 s, p90 1.84 s, maximum 2.54 s; zero fallbacks in the 12 replies played through the game. The whole run is estimated below one cent. This sample does not justify raising limits and is not a human playtest. See [the live report](live-model-results-2026-11-15.md).
